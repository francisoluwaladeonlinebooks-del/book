# Chapter 10: Metabolism, muscle and midlife

Fatima gained weight after her periods stopped. She ate roughly the same food and walked the same route. A clinician told her to eat less and move more. She did both for six months and regained only some of what she had lost.

Body composition changes across midlife, and weight is a poor proxy for what is happening in muscle, bone and glucose regulation. Figures about muscle loss and insulin resistance often arrive without a population or a method. The same is true of a fixed protein rule tied to a birthday.

## Energy balance is real and incomplete

Body weight changes when energy intake and energy use change over time. That statement is useful and limited. It says nothing about appetite, medication, sleep, pain, disability, menopausal symptoms or the fact that weighing food for a month is not a life.

Muscle mass tends to decline with age, and the rate varies between individuals. Resistance training and enough protein help preserve it. Adult guidelines recommend muscle-strengthening activity on two or more days a week, alongside 150 to 300 minutes of moderate aerobic activity [1].

## Protein without a magic percentage

The RDA for healthy adults is 0.8 grams per kilogram of body weight a day [2]. Expert groups working with older adults recommend around 1.0 to 1.2 g/kg/day for healthy people over 65, and more in illness or with diagnosed sarcopenia [3]. These are ranges and starting points. They are not a rule that flips on at a birthday, and higher intakes need care in kidney disease.

For a 70 kg person, 0.8 g/kg is 56 g a day and 1.2 g/kg is 84 g. Distributed across meals, that is an ordinary amount of food for many diets. Ask a clinician or dietitian if you have a medical reason to restrict protein.

## Insulin resistance in plain language

Insulin helps move glucose from the blood into cells. Insulin resistance means cells respond less well, so the body compensates with more insulin. Over time, this can precede type 2 diabetes. Risk rises with family history, higher body weight, low activity, some medications and sleep disruption.

Clinicians use tests such as fasting glucose and HbA1c to assess glucose handling; treatment thresholds are set by diagnostic guidelines [4]. A private fasting-insulin number without a clinical context is not a diagnosis.

## Intermittent fasting is optional

Time-restricted eating and other fasting patterns can produce weight loss in some adults, largely because people eat less overall. Reviews find benefit in some groups, but women were under-represented in much of the early research [5].

Avoid fasting altogether, or seek specific medical advice first, if you are pregnant or breastfeeding, have a current or past eating disorder, take insulin or sulfonylureas, have diabetes, or are underweight. Menstrual changes, persistent fatigue, anxiety and sleep disturbance are reasons to stop and review.

A 12-hour overnight eating window is a gentler starting point than a 16- or 18-hour fast. It is also ordinary enough to maintain.

## Fatima's revised plan

With a dietitian, Fatima added two short strength sessions a week, kept her walking, aimed for protein at each meal and stopped skipping lunch. She did not fast. Her weight moved slowly. What changed more was her ability to carry groceries and her confidence in her own plan. The composite story ends with a blood result and a conversation, not a before-and-after photograph.

## Questions to ask

- Given my age and medical history, what protein target makes sense for me?
- Do my glucose results place me in a normal, increased-risk or diabetic range?
- Would a referral to a dietitian or a pelvic-floor and strength professional help?
- Could any of my medicines be affecting weight or appetite?

## The standing record

Write down one strength activity you could do twice a week for the next month. Note the date, what you did, and how you felt two hours later.
