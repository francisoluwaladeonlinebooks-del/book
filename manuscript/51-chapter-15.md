# Chapter 15: The next generation

Amara was twelve when her mother sat down with a calendar and a packet of pencils. The composite lesson was simple: mark the first day of bleeding, count the days to the next one, and write down how the week felt. No lecture, no diagram of a perfect 28-day cycle.

A young person needs skills more than statistics.

## At twelve: body literacy

A twelve-year-old needs a few concrete ideas.

Bleeding is a normal process, and pain that stops her doing ordinary things is worth reporting. Cycles vary, especially in the first years after they begin. A period is not a mark of shame and does not require silence. Menstruation is not the same as being fertile on a predictable schedule.

Practical skills matter more than vocabulary: how to use a pad or cup, how to change it, how to wash a stained item, how to ask a trusted adult for help, and when a symptom is urgent.

## At sixteen: understanding options

Sixteen is a good age for accurate information about contraception. Hormonal methods are tools with benefits and side effects; they do not cure PCOS, endometriosis or acne in the way a course of treatment might. Some suppress bleeding. That can be useful, and it is different from resolving the underlying condition [1].

Sexual health education works best when it includes consent, boundaries and where to get confidential advice. Fertility apps should be described honestly: they are not reliable contraception [2].

A young person with persistent pelvic pain, very heavy bleeding or cycles that have stopped should be assessed. Pain that is dismissed as "normal periods" is a common route into years of delay.

## At twenty: prevention and records

By early adulthood, many people are managing their own appointments for the first time. Useful habits include keeping a medicine list, knowing the date of the last cervical screening test if applicable, knowing family history, and understanding what a normal result means for a specific test.

University and work bring shift patterns, stress, new food routines and new sexual partners. A short record of changes helps a clinician see what happened. Body literacy at this age includes knowing that a missed period has several possible causes and that a home pregnancy test has limits.

## Raising boys without a script of disbelief

Boys need the same basic facts. They should know that menstrual pain can be disabling, that people vary, and that a claim of pain is not a negotiation. They also need permission to report their own symptoms rather than performing toughness.

In the composite story, Amara's mother spoke to her son in plain language. Years later he was the person who drove a friend to an appointment when she could not. Nothing in that story required a statistic.

## What schools can realistically teach

A school programme does not need to turn teenagers into clinicians. A workable minimum includes the range of normal cycle variation, how to seek help, what contraception does and does not do, and how to challenge a dismissive response respectfully. Teachers need training and clear referral routes; a lesson without a route to help leaves a student with information and nowhere to use it.

Curricula differ by country and are often politically contested. Check what is taught locally and ask what is missing.

## The standing record

If you are teaching a young person, write down three things you wish someone had told you at their age. If you are the young person, write down three questions you have never had a good answer to.
