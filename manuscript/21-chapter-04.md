# Chapter 4: Your body's operating system

In the story, Zara is a doctor in training who keeps a systems notebook. The character is a composite, not a researcher with a named team.

In the story, Zara is a doctor in training whose periods stop during a stretch of night shifts and heavy exercise. She eats more, sleeps when she can, reduces training and seeks supervision. Her cycle returns over several months. Nothing in that sequence is a protocol; it is a reminder that energy availability, stress and circadian disruption can affect ovulation [1].

## Five systems as a thinking tool

The framework uses five labels:

- The hormonal system: signalling through the endocrine glands.
- The metabolic system: how the body obtains and uses energy.
- The nervous system: stress response, autonomic regulation and recovery.
- The immune system: defence, inflammation and repair.
- The circadian system: the timing of sleep, light exposure and daily rhythms.

These are not five separate boxes. They exchange signals, and the same hormone can be involved in more than one of them. Cortisol participates in the stress response and in metabolism. Sleep affects glucose handling and immune function. Thyroid hormones influence energy use, mood and temperature regulation.

Earlier drafts claimed that women's systems are uniquely interconnected while men's operate independently. That claim overstated the case. Human physiology uses the same axes in all bodies; the timing and the research questions differ. What matters for this book is practical: changing one habit often affects more than one signal you are tracking.

## Feedback loops are worth knowing about

A systems view pays attention to loops.

Sleep loss can increase hunger and reduce the energy available for exercise. Less movement can worsen glucose control, which can affect sleep. A run of poor nights can make pain feel stronger, which makes sleep harder again.

Under-eating during heavy training can disrupt ovulation. Irregular cycles can cause worry, and worry can add to the stress load. A clinician who only sees the worry has missed the training load.

An inflammatory flare can reduce activity and sleep. Reduced sleep can worsen pain sensitivity. Treating the flare, the sleep or the pain alone may leave the loop partly intact.

None of these sketches is a universal explanation. They are examples of how to ask "what else changed?" instead of looking for one culprit.

## The twelve signals

A 12-item checklist can help organise observations. It has no validated score or cut-offs, and the numbers carry no diagnostic meaning.

The twelve signals are kept here as a checklist. Rate each from 1 to 10 if you want, but treat the number as a note to yourself.

1. Sleep quality: falling asleep, staying asleep, waking rested.
2. Energy stability: steady through the day or dependent on caffeine and sugar.
3. Mood: the usual range for you, including irritability and anxiety.
4. Thinking: concentration and word-finding.
5. Stress capacity: what you can absorb before you feel overwhelmed.
6. Digestion: comfort, bloating and regularity.
7. Cycle: interval between periods and the severity of symptoms, if you menstruate.
8. Recovery after exercise or illness.
9. Libido: change from your own baseline.
10. Weight and body composition: change without an obvious reason.
11. Skin and hair: changes that concern you.
12. Temperature: hot flushes, cold intolerance or sweating that is new.

A low number is a prompt for a question. It is not a diagnosis. A high number on eleven items and a low one on sleep points to sleep; a scattered pattern across many items may deserve a broader clinical review.

## From labels to questions

The revised framework has four steps.

Step one: record what is happening, with dates.

Step two: look for a change in timing. Did the symptom start after a new medicine, a shift-work block, a move, a bereavement or a change in training?

Step three: choose one or two low-risk habits to test for a month. Keep everything else as steady as you can.

Step four: decide what you would need to see to know the habit helped. If nothing changed, write that down too: a null result is useful information.

This is closer to how an engineer tests a hypothesis than to a wellness challenge. 
## What the framework cannot do

It cannot tell you whether a thyroid result needs treatment. It cannot separate anxiety from a hormone-related mood change on its own. It cannot tell you whether a pain is urgent. It cannot replace an examination.

The value of the framework is narrower: it keeps details from being lost, and it makes the questions you bring to a professional easier to answer.

## The standing record

Choose the three signals that have changed most in the last month. Write one sentence about each, including a date. Leave the score out of it.
