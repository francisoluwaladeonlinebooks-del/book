Author's note: why a systems person wrote a book about health

The first version of this book claimed more than it could support. It described a research bureau, a large original dataset and a set of outcome percentages. I could not produce the records behind any of it. I rebuilt the book around what can be checked.

That decision changed the shape of the book. It removed the idea that a set of habits can reverse a diagnosis. It removed the claim that a private dataset had settled questions that clinical trials have not settled. What remained was a more modest and more useful idea: that health information, organised well, helps people ask better questions.

My background is in computer science and information technology. I have worked as an IT manager, and I have trained in data analysis and visualisation, including data analytics through Cisco and the 3MTT programme. None of that qualifies me to interpret a lab result or prescribe a treatment. It does qualify me to think about structure: what gets recorded, what gets lost, how feedback loops behave, and how a small change in one part of a system shows up elsewhere.

The people who should judge the medical content are clinicians. A blank review packet for that work is included with the editorial material that accompanies this manuscript. Until a qualified reviewer has completed it, this book should be read as education written by a careful non-specialist.

If you take one thing from the book, let it be this: keep your own record, ask precise questions, and let a qualified professional help you interpret both.
