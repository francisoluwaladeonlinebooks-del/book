# Chapter 13: The economics of care

A health economist in the composite story sat with her daughter after a delayed diagnosis and started adding up the costs. Not the dramatic headline figures, but the ordinary ones: repeat appointments, time off work, transport, a prescription that helped, a prescription that did not.

The headline figure is public and its assumptions are stated. Smaller national totals circulate without a clear source.

## The number that is public

The World Economic Forum and McKinsey Health Institute estimated that closing the women's health gap could add at least $1 trillion to annual global GDP by 2040 [1]. The same reporting notes that women spend about 25% more of their lives in poor health than men and that women's health receives a small share of health research and development funding, around 5% in one widely cited estimate [2].

These are modelled figures. They describe what might happen if treatment, access and data improved. They are not a bill that any country has received, and they say nothing about what an individual woman should spend.

## Why conditions stay underfunded

Diseases that mainly affect women have historically received less research attention. The 1993 US requirement that federally funded trials include women came late, and the gap in preclinical research lasted longer [3]. Conditions with variable symptoms and no single diagnostic test are harder to study and harder to fund.

A chronic condition that is managed rather than resolved also creates a market for repeated management. That observation is common in health-policy writing. It is a criticism of incentives, not evidence that any clinician deliberately withholds a cure.

## The personal budget

Health costs fall into three buckets:

- Costs you can anticipate: consultations, transport, childcare, a portion of prescriptions.
- Costs you can reduce: duplicate tests if records are carried, missed appointments if reminders are set, penalties for late cancellation.
- Costs you should not carry alone: emergency care, invasive investigations and treatment decisions that need a second opinion.

A simple spreadsheet with columns for date, item, amount and whether it was necessary is enough. If a condition affects your work, keep a separate note of days missed. That note can support a conversation with an employer about adjustments.

## Insurance and coverage

Coverage rules vary so widely between countries and plans that this book cannot give a usable set of instructions. What transfers between systems is the method: read the policy, ask for the list of excluded conditions, ask whether a referral is required before a specialist visit, and get a written answer when a claim is refused. Keep copies of everything.

For conditions with long diagnostic delays, a record of symptom dates and previous appointments is often more useful than a single current letter.

## Femtech and its limits

Cycle apps, symptom trackers and at-home test kits can help people notice patterns. Most are classed as wellness products, not medical devices. They are not required to prove that their predictions are accurate. Fertile-window predictions from common apps are unreliable enough that they should not be used to prevent pregnancy [4].

Data is another consideration. Read the privacy policy. Know whether the company sells de-identified data or shares it with insurers or employers. If you would not send the information on a postcard, check the settings.

## The composite version of a fund

A personal support fund is a question rather than a programme. If a condition is likely to need three specialist visits and a year of treatment, what practical support would make that possible? The answer might be a family rota, a workplace adjustment, a savings habit or a charity that pays transport costs. None of that replaces a functioning health service.

## Questions to ask about cost

- What is the likely total cost of this plan, including follow-up?
- Which parts are covered, and which need my approval first?
- Is there a cheaper option with a similar expected benefit?
- What happens if we do nothing for three months?

## The standing record

Write down the last three health-related costs you paid. Mark which ones you expected. Use that list at your next appointment when asking about a plan.
