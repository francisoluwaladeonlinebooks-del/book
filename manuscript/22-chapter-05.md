# Chapter 5: Light, sleep and biological timing

Stories about moon cycles and ovulation are appealing. Human ovulation is not switched on by moonlight, and the reported synchrony figures do not survive careful review.

The chapter also claimed that shift work damaged women's health 40% faster than men's. I could not verify the comparison. What I could verify is enough to take timing seriously.

## The clock you can influence

The circadian system coordinates sleep, alertness, hormone release and body temperature across roughly 24 hours. Light is its strongest external signal. Bright light early in the day supports alertness and helps anchor the body clock. Light late at night, especially blue-enriched light close to the eyes, suppresses melatonin and can delay sleep [1].

Melatonin is a signal of darkness, not a sedative in the pharmacological sense. Its timing matters more than a single number from a saliva test sold online.

A practical routine follows from this:

- Get outside within an hour of waking when you can. Ten to twenty minutes is enough for many people; longer is fine.
- Keep wake times roughly consistent, including at weekends.
- Lower the light level in the last hour before bed and put bright screens away from the face.
- Keep the bedroom cool, dark and quiet.

These are ordinary sleep-hygiene steps with plausible mechanisms. They are not a treatment for insomnia, and persistent insomnia deserves assessment rather than another lamp.

## Sleep duration and women

Adults need at least seven hours of sleep on a regular basis [2]. Women report more insomnia and more unrefreshing sleep than men. Higher rates of mood disturbance, pain conditions, caregiving interruptions and sleep apnoea that goes undetected all contribute. The answer is not a different universal number; it is paying attention to why the sleep is broken.

Night sweats during perimenopause can fragment sleep. If you wake damp and hot several nights a week, record that separately from "poor sleep". It points to a specific question about vasomotor symptoms and their treatment options.

## Shift work is a real exposure

The International Agency for Research on Cancer classifies night shift work as probably carcinogenic to humans, Group 2A [3]. That classification is about the strength of hazard evidence, not the size of an individual's risk. Human evidence was described as limited, with positive associations observed for breast, prostate and colorectal cancers.

Shift work is also associated with fatigue, disturbed sleep and metabolic changes. If your work involves nights, the useful interventions are at the level of the roster and the environment: consistent blocks rather than rapid rotations where possible, protected dark sleep time, light management on the commute home, and a lower threshold for asking about sleep and mood symptoms.

## Cycle variability is normal; a fixed 28-day script is not

Cycles commonly range from about 21 to 35 days in adults, and they vary from month to month. Ovulation does not reliably happen on day 14. This is why fertility apps that rely on calendar predictions should not be used as contraception [4]. It is also why a rhythm practice built around a perfect 28-day chart will mislead people whose cycles are longer, shorter or irregular.

Track your own intervals for three months before drawing conclusions. If cycles were regular and have become erratic, add that to your notes.

## The rhythm reset, stated modestly

The "Rhythm Reset" in this book is a four-part routine: morning daylight, a consistent wake time, a darker evening and a record of how you slept. It was assembled from public health guidance. It has no proprietary algorithm, no clinical trial and no outcome guarantee.

In the composite story, a software engineer with irregular periods tried the routine for eight weeks while also reducing an unsustainable workload and seeing a clinician. Her cycle settled. The story illustrates a process of testing several changes at once. It does not show that the routine caused the change.

## Questions for an appointment

- I wake at 3 a.m. several nights a week. What would you want to know before calling this insomnia?
- Could night sweats be breaking my sleep, and what options are there if they are?
- Does my shift pattern change how you interpret these symptoms?
- If I use an app to track cycles, which parts of its output are useful to you?

## The standing record

For seven days, note the time you went outside, the time you stopped looking at bright screens, and your estimate of sleep quality. Do not change anything yet. Observe first.
