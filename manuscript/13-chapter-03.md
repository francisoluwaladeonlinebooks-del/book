# Chapter 3: Burnout, stress and hormones

Lin managed a team that built financial models. She was good at holding several complex problems in her head at once. Then the holding became harder. She reread paragraphs. She forgot the name of a colleague she had worked with for years. By Friday she could still do the work, but it took twice as long.

Her doctor found nothing unusual in a standard blood panel and suggested a holiday. Lin took one. The fog lifted for four days and returned the moment she was back at her desk.

This composite captures a common frustration: the symptoms are real, the routine tests are often normal, and the explanation offered is whichever one is easiest to say in a six-minute consultation.

## What stress does in the body, and what it does not

The hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, usually shortened to HPA axis, links the brain to the adrenal glands. A stressor triggers a sequence of signals that ends in cortisol release. Cortisol follows a daily rhythm: higher on waking, declining across the day. Repeated activation of this system can affect sleep, appetite, mood and glucose regulation. Researchers use the term allostatic load for the cumulative cost of repeated adaptation [1].

That is a real physiology. It does not follow that a tired woman has a broken hormone system. Burnout is defined by the World Health Organization as an occupational phenomenon, characterised by exhaustion, cynicism or mental distance from work, and reduced professional efficacy. WHO explicitly does not classify it as a medical condition [2]. Depression, anxiety, thyroid disease, anaemia, sleep apnoea and autoimmune disease can all look like burnout from the outside.

A single fatigue score cannot identify the cause of fatigue. A panel of cortisol samples taken at home cannot turn a symptom into a diagnosis. Online percentage sets that claim to find hormone dysfunction in most burnt-out women have no published method attached.

## "Adrenal fatigue" is not a diagnosis

The term appears constantly in wellness writing. It is not recognised by endocrinology societies. A 2016 systematic review found no evidence that healthy adrenal glands become exhausted by ordinary stress, and no validated test for the proposed state [3]. The label is sometimes used to sell supplements or staged hormone protocols.

The person using the label may still feel dreadful. The fatigue, the wired feeling at night and the difficulty concentrating are real experiences. The explanation on the packet may be wrong. Persistent symptoms deserve an ordinary clinical assessment: history, examination, relevant blood tests, and attention to sleep and mood. Adrenal insufficiency is a genuine and serious condition; it is diagnosed with specific tests and treated with replacement hormones under specialist care.

## Sleep is a system input

Adults are advised to sleep at least seven hours a night on a regular basis [4]. Women report more disturbed sleep than men and more insomnia, while the official duration guidance does not set a separate female target. Menopause, pregnancy, caregiving and mood all affect the hours actually obtained.

There is a useful distinction here between time in bed and sleep quality. Someone in bed for eight hours who wakes four times has had a different night from someone asleep for seven hours without interruption. If snoring, gasping, morning headaches or daytime sleepiness are part of the picture, ask about sleep apnoea rather than buying another pillow.

## The composite recovery

Lin's recovery, in the composite version of this story, was unremarkable. She reduced her working week, walked outside in the morning, protected one evening a week, and went to a clinician when the fog persisted. She was found to have iron deficiency and a sleep problem that responded to treatment. Her energy improved over months. There was no single protocol and no dramatic night after which everything changed.

I keep this story deliberately plain. Accounts of sudden total recovery after a 30-day plan make good advertising and poor medical education.

## A better way to describe burnout to a clinician

Write three lines before the appointment:

- The function that changed: concentration, memory, stamina, decision-making.
- The timeline: when it started, whether it is constant, and what makes it better or worse.
- The associated changes: sleep, appetite, mood, cycle, temperature, pain.

Then say which of these you would like checked first. That gives the clinician a differential to work through instead of a single word that has several meanings.

## When the answer is not stress

Low mood with loss of interest, changes in appetite or sleep, and difficulty functioning deserve a mental health assessment. Suicidal thoughts require urgent help. Unexplained weight change, persistent fever, night sweats, fainting or a new severe headache are not stress symptoms to be managed with a breathing app.

## The standing record

For seven nights, record bedtime, estimated sleep time, number of wake-ups and how you felt at noon. Bring the pattern, not the average, to your appointment.
