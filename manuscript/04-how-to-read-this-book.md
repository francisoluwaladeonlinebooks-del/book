How to read this book

I work in information technology. My training is in computer science, and most of my working life has been spent on systems, data and the question of how people make decisions with incomplete information. That is the perspective I bring here. I am not bringing clinical authority, and this book does not pretend to have it.

The problem I saw repeatedly was one of organisation. A woman notices a pattern in her body. She arrives at an appointment with a head full of events and a few weeks' worth of memory. The appointment lasts minutes. The pattern disappears in the telling.

A system can help with that. A simple record, kept consistently, can turn scattered observations into something another person can read. It can also help the woman reading it see that her symptoms cluster around a change in sleep, a phase of her cycle, a new medication or a period of sustained stress.

This book is built around five parts. The first two describe the gap in care and give you a way to organise what you observe. The third part offers ordinary routines around light, food, movement and recovery. The fourth part deals with appointments, costs and support. The last part looks at education and public policy.

Three rules run through the whole book.

First, a pattern in your own life is useful evidence for a conversation. It is not a diagnosis.

Second, a statistically sound result from a population tells you something about groups. It cannot tell you, on its own, what is happening in one body.

Third, a habit that is low risk and reasonable for one person can be unsuitable for another. Pregnancy, a history of disordered eating, kidney disease, diabetes and prescription medication all change the calculation.

Every story in this book is a composite. Names, jobs, cities and details have been assembled from common patterns described in published literature. None of these people is a real patient, and none of the stories is clinical evidence.

Numbers in the text point to a source in the reference section at the back. Sources are listed by chapter. Where a number comes from a model rather than a measurement, I say so. Where I could not verify a claim, I left it out.

The Standing Record at the end of each chapter is an invitation to write something down. It is not a test with a pass mark.
