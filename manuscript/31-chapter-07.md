# Chapter 7: The foundation routine

Sarah wanted one change. She had tried a complete health overhaul twice and abandoned it both times within a fortnight. The composite version of her story is deliberately ordinary: she chose morning light for a month, added a breakfast habit in the second month, and kept both through the winter.

Common claims about this kind of routine promise percentage changes in sleep, serotonin and melatonin. Those figures have no traceable study behind them.

What remains is a set of four low-risk habits drawn from general public-health guidance. They are worth trying because they are cheap and compatible with most lives. They are not a treatment for PCOS, thyroid disease, anxiety, insomnia or perimenopause.

## Week 1: morning daylight

Aim for ten to twenty minutes outside within an hour of waking. On a bright day, sit near a window if going outside is impossible, though outdoor light is stronger. The goal is a consistent morning signal to the body clock [1].

This habit suits shift workers less neatly. If you sleep during the day, "morning" means the start of your waking period. Keep the principle and adjust the clock.

## Week 2: a protein-containing breakfast

Adult protein needs are usually expressed per kilogram of body weight. The RDA for healthy adults is 0.8 g/kg/day [2]. A breakfast with a substantial protein source can help with satiety and with distributing protein across the day, which matters more for older adults and people in strength training.

Practical examples: eggs, yoghurt, beans, tofu, fish or leftover meat with a familiar carbohydrate. There is no requirement to eat within one hour of waking and no evidence from this book that a particular breakfast changes hormone levels by a measurable percentage.

If you have kidney disease or a condition that restricts protein, ask your clinician before increasing intake.

## Week 3: short breaks

Three times a day, take three to five minutes away from the task. Stand up, look at something distant, walk a few steps or breathe slowly without trying to force a number. Regular breaks can reduce the feeling of being stuck, and movement breaks interrupt long periods of sitting, which public-health guidance advises [3].

You can call these breaks circuit breakers if you like. Use them because they are workable, and leave claims about cortisol charts to studies that measured it.

## Week 4: a darker evening

Lower the lights in the last hour before bed. Move bright screens away from your face or use a setting that reduces blue light. Keep the habit modest enough to survive a normal week [1].

If you share a room with someone who needs light, a sleep mask and a dim lamp may be a better compromise than a strict rule.

## Stacking without a scoreboard

Add one habit at a time. Keep it for four weeks. Write down two things: how consistently you did it and whether anything you were already tracking changed.

Sarah's composite notes read like this: "Week 1: outside on 22 of 28 days. Sleep about the same. Week 2: breakfast most days, less mid-morning hunger. Week 4: fell asleep earlier on days without a late screen." That is the level of detail that helps.

## Where people get stuck

Night shifts, early caregiving, food insecurity and small living spaces change what is possible. A habit that requires a gym membership, a meditation app subscription or a particular kitchen gadget is not a foundation habit. Choose the version that fits your week.

Some people feel worse when they add several habits at once. If that happens, drop back to the one that felt easiest.

## When a habit is not enough

Four weeks of daylight and breakfast will not settle heavy bleeding, new chest pain, a persistent fever, major low mood or a cycle that has stopped for months. Those need clinical assessment. Self-care routines work best alongside appropriate care, not instead of it.

## The standing record

Pick one of the four habits. Write the version you can do on your worst week, not your best week. Try it for 28 days and record consistency only.
