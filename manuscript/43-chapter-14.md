# Chapter 14: Building your bureau

Maya called her circle a bureau because it kept records. Five people shared a document: practitioners they had seen, questions that worked, tests that had been useful and clinicians who had listened. No one in the group told the others what to take.

Support helps people feel less isolated and more prepared. Any claim about a fixed improvement in outcomes would need evidence this book does not have.

## The word bureau

Throughout the book, "Bureau Protocol" names a way of organising information. It does not name an organisation, and the book has no affiliated clinics, researchers or research programme. In this chapter, "your bureau" means the small group of people and records you build around your own care.

A personal bureau has three parts: a record, a set of questions, and people you trust to help you think.

## Building a care team

Different problems call for different people. A general practitioner or primary-care clinician often coordinates. A gynaecologist, endocrinologist, dermatologist or rheumatologist may be needed for a specific condition. A pelvic-floor physical therapist can help with pain and urinary symptoms. A registered dietitian is useful when food is part of the plan and money is tight. A therapist or counsellor can help when a condition has changed your life.

A peer mentor who has been through a similar diagnosis can explain what appointments are like. That experience is valuable. It does not confer clinical knowledge, and advice about medication belongs with a prescriber.

Write down what each person's role is. When roles are unclear, the same question gets asked to three people and answered by none.

## Online groups

Online communities are good at sharing practical knowledge: which clinic has a long waiting list, how to phrase a request for records, what a procedure felt like. They are poor places to interpret an individual's lab results. Stories of recovery are selected, not representative.

If a group starts selling a supplement protocol with a promise of reversal, treat that as a commercial transaction. Ask who profits and what evidence they have.

## Intergenerational conversations

Older women often carry hard-won practical knowledge: which questions got answers, which hospital was worth travelling to, which treatment had side effects. Younger women bring familiarity with online records and newer treatments. Both sides have something to offer.

A conversation can be useful without a percentage attached.

## A structure that stays small

A workable peer group needs only a few rules:

- Meet with a purpose: one topic, one hour.
- Share what was useful, including what was not.
- Keep medical details private unless the person offers them.
- Never tell another member to stop or start a medicine.
- Allow people to leave the group without explanation.

The last rule matters. Chronic illness changes energy, and a group that demands constant participation becomes another demand.

## When you need more than peers

Peer support cannot assess chest pain, manage a severe flare or treat depression. Keep a separate list of urgent contacts: emergency number, local crisis line, the clinic's advice line and the person who can drive you. Put it on paper as well as in your phone.

## The standing record

Write the names and roles of the three people currently involved in your care. Add one gap you would like to fill in the next month.
