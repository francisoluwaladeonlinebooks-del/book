# Chapter 1: The silence in the exam room

Maria worked eighteen years in an emergency department. She could walk into a bay, look at a patient's colour and the set of their shoulders, and start assembling the likely causes of chest pain before the monitor printed a rhythm. When her own palpitations began, she wrote them off as a long week.

The palpitations continued. So did the breathlessness on the stairs at home and the waves of heat that had nothing to do with the weather. She had one appointment where the clinician glanced at the results, said the tests were normal, and suggested anxiety. Maria did not argue. She left, sat in the car and went through her own notes again.

This story is a composite. It is not a case report. It is included because the pattern is documented: women's accounts of pain and physical symptoms are more likely to be treated with suspicion, and the difference shows up in what they are prescribed.

## What the studies actually show

A 2024 study in *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* examined 21,851 emergency-department records from the United States and Israel. Female patients with pain complaints were less likely to receive a prescription for pain relief than male patients. Their pain score was less likely to be recorded at triage, and they spent about 30 minutes longer in the department. The pattern held at every pain score and in every age group in the data. In a separate experiment in the same study, nurses given identical case vignettes rated a female patient's pain as less intense when the case said the patient was female [1].

An older prospective study from 2008 followed 981 patients with acute abdominal pain. Men and women reported similar pain scores. Women were less likely to receive opioid pain relief, and those who did receive it waited 65 minutes on average, compared with 49 minutes for men [2].

Those numbers are not proof that every clinician is biased. They are evidence that a bias exists at the level of a system, and that a system-level bias can be measured. That is a sufficiently serious finding without adding figures that nobody can source.

The phrase "medical gaslighting" is now used for the experience of having symptoms minimised or attributed to mood. It is a useful description of what patients report, not a diagnosis and not a legal finding. I use it as shorthand for a pattern of interaction, not as an accusation against a named person.

## The research base was thinner than it should have been

Until 1993, federally funded clinical trials in the United States were not required to include women. The NIH Revitalization Act changed that requirement. Preclinical research lagged further behind: a survey of 2009 animal studies found single-sex male studies outnumbered single-sex female studies by 5.5 to 1 in neuroscience, and similar gaps appeared in pharmacology and physiology [3, 4]. In 2014, the NIH announced a policy requiring both sexes in the preclinical research it funds [5].

The practical consequence is not that nothing is known about women's health. It is that some assumptions were carried forward without being tested in female bodies. A drug dose, a diagnostic cut-off or a symptom description may have been inherited from a study population that excluded half of the people now being treated.

## What dismissal looks like in a room

It rarely looks like a doctor saying "it is all in your head". More often it is a sequence of small moves.

A symptom is not written down. A normal result is treated as a complete answer even when the question was about something else. An emotional reaction to pain becomes the explanation for the pain. A treatment that helped a previous patient with similar words is offered without checking whether the underlying problem is the same.

Some of these moves have reasonable explanations. An emergency department prioritises the immediately dangerous. A test ordered for one question may not answer another. A clinician working through a crowded list has minutes, not hours. Understanding the pressure does not make the experience less real, but it helps to separate two things: a clinician who is rushed, and a pattern of under-taking symptoms seriously.

## What you can do in the appointment

A record gives the conversation something specific to work with, even when nobody changes their mind.

Say what happened, when it happened and what changed. "Palpitations most evenings for six weeks, lasting about ten minutes, worse after climbing the stairs" gives a clinician more to work with than "I feel awful".

Bring the log, not an argument. A one-page summary with dates, symptoms and a short list of questions is easier to use than a month of unfiltered notes.

Ask what the normal result does and does not rule out. Ask whether the symptom pattern fits any condition that was not tested for. Ask what would change the plan.

Repeat the question if it was not answered. A second, calmer ask often gets a fuller reply than an immediate correction.

Write down the answer. It is easy to leave an appointment convinced you were agreed with and discover later that nobody committed to anything.

## When the stakes are higher

Chest pain, fainting, sudden weakness, difficulty breathing, severe abdominal or pelvic pain, heavy bleeding and thoughts of self-harm need urgent assessment regardless of what a record shows. If a clinician has previously dismissed your symptoms, that history is a reason to be explicit about the new symptom, not a reason to stay away from emergency care.

## The standing record

For one week, write down each physical symptom that interrupted something you were doing. Include the time, the duration and one possible trigger. Bring the page to your next appointment and read it aloud if you need to.
