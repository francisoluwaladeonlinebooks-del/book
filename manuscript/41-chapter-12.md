# Chapter 12: Changing clinicians

Emma spent seven years with painful periods before a gynaecologist took the pattern seriously. In the composite story, the first clinician said that painful periods were common and suggested a heavier painkiller. The second asked about bowel symptoms, examined her, arranged imaging and discussed endometriosis.

WHO estimates that endometriosis affects about 10% of reproductive-age women and reports an average diagnostic delay of 4 to 12 years [1]. The delay has several causes: symptoms vary, pain is normalised, and a definitive diagnosis sometimes needs imaging or surgery. This is a system problem that an individual can navigate but cannot solve alone.

Recording may help you remember; a companion may help you ask. Neither changes a clinician's knowledge.

## Recognising a poor fit

Signs that a working relationship is not helping:

- Questions about the pattern of symptoms are cut off before the pattern is described.
- A normal result is offered as the end of the enquiry when the question was about something else.
- New symptoms are attributed to anxiety without an explanation or assessment.
- You leave without knowing what was agreed, what was ordered, or when to follow up.
- Requests for your records or for a referral are repeatedly obstructed.

One bad appointment is not proof of a bad clinician. A repeated pattern over several visits is a reason to change course.

## Before the next appointment

Write four lines:

1. The main symptom and how long it has been present.
2. The pattern: times of day, cycle days, triggers, what eases it.
3. What has already been tried and what happened.
4. The two questions you most need answered.

Take the medicines list with doses. Take the journal, if you have one, and a short summary rather than every page.

## Scripts that stay civil

"I have written down the pattern because I find it hard to explain under time pressure. May I read two lines?"

"When you say the test is normal, does that rule out the thing I am worried about, or does it mean we need a different test?"

"I understand that anxiety can change how pain feels. What would you want to check before treating this as anxiety?"

"Could you write down what we have agreed, including the follow-up date?"

"What would make you change your mind about this explanation?"

If the reply is dismissive, repeating the question once in different words is reasonable. Continuing to argue rarely is.

## Second opinions

A second opinion is a normal part of care, not an accusation. It is especially useful when a proposed treatment is irreversible, when symptoms are getting worse without an explanation, or when a diagnosis has been given without tests you can see.

Bring the records. Ask for the reasoning behind the first clinician's plan, and ask what the second clinician would do differently. You can then choose with more information. A fifth opinion may be reasonable for a rare condition; it may also be a sign that the question being asked is too broad. Narrow it.

## Records, consent and the law

In many jurisdictions patients have a right to access their medical records and to give or refuse consent for treatment. Rules on recording appointments, consent for sharing records and complaints procedures differ by country and by state. Ask the clinic's patient-advice office or your national health regulator for the local process. Nothing in this book is legal advice.

If you record an appointment, say so. Keep the file secure and use it to check your own understanding.

## Telehealth and where it falls short

Remote consultations can be useful for follow-up, results and prescriptions that do not require examination. New pelvic pain, abnormal bleeding, severe headaches and anything resembling an emergency usually need an in-person assessment. Online clinics vary in quality; check who is licensed and where they are licensed.

## The composite ending

Emma changed clinicians twice. The second change was not a miracle. It produced a clear plan, a date for review and a clinician who read the journal she had kept. That is a reasonable outcome to aim for.

## The standing record

Write the two questions you want answered at your next appointment. Put the more important one first.
