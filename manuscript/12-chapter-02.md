# Chapter 2: The invisible lifecycle

Aisha was 39 when anger started arriving without a warning. It came in the middle of meetings and on the drive home. She snapped at people she liked. Then came the heat at night, the thickened silence of a period that arrived late, and a fatigue that a weekend of sleep did not touch.

Her doctor suggested work stress. Aisha had work stress, and she had a deadline that would have undone most people. That explanation was plausible enough that she accepted it for eight months. What finally changed her mind was a pattern she noticed while keeping a simple sleep diary: the worst nights clustered in the ten days before her period.

This is a composite story. Perimenopause varies so widely that two people with the same age can have entirely different experiences. That variation is the point. There is no single timetable, and no symptom list that applies to everyone.

## Three transitions that get confused

Irregular cycles in the years before menopause often reflect changing ovulation. Hot flushes and night sweats are common vasomotor symptoms, but their frequency and duration differ between individuals and populations. Sleep disturbance in midlife can come from night sweats, from mood, from an untreated sleep disorder such as sleep apnoea, or from all three at once.

PCOS is a different thing. It is a long-term hormonal and metabolic condition, usually diagnosed when oligo-ovulation, clinical or biochemical hyperandrogenism, and polycystic ovaries are present in the right combination after other causes have been excluded. WHO puts the estimate at 10 to 13% of reproductive-age women, with the figure changing with the criteria used and the population studied [1]. Up to 70% of affected women may be undiagnosed, according to the same source.

Endometriosis is another condition with a long road to diagnosis. WHO estimates that it affects about 10%, or 190 million reproductive-age women worldwide, and reports an average diagnostic delay of between 4 and 12 years [2]. Pain that disrupts life is not a requirement a patient has to meet before she is allowed to ask questions.

These three situations overlap in midlife. A person can have PCOS and enter perimenopause. A person can have endometriosis and reach the years when cycles become irregular. A label should help organise care; it should not be used to stop looking when symptoms change.

## Why "too young" is a weak answer

Age alone cannot rule out a symptom's cause. Menopause before age 40 is called premature ovarian insufficiency and needs proper assessment and long-term management. Irregular periods at 24 deserve a different work-up from irregular periods at 48. In both cases, the pattern, the symptoms and the relevant tests matter more than a rough age band.

Some symptoms are common enough to be unremarkable and still worth recording. A cycle that ranges from 23 to 35 days can be normal. A cycle that was regular for years and has become unpredictable, or bleeding after intercourse, or a sudden change in flow, deserves assessment [3].

## The cost is real; the precise figure is harder

The World Economic Forum and McKinsey Health Institute modelled the economic gains from closing the women's health gap. Their 2024 report estimated that better women's health could add at least $1 trillion to annual global GDP by 2040 and reduce the 25% excess time women spend in poor health [4]. That is a projection based on assumptions about treatment, access and productivity. It is not an invoice for a single year and it is not a measure of any one woman's loss.

The report is public and its assumptions are stated. Smaller national figures circulate without a clear source and are best left out.

## A life-stage map you can actually use

A life-stage map is more useful than a curve. Use four columns:

1. Life stage: menstruating, trying to conceive, pregnant or breastfeeding, perimenopausal, postmenopausal.
2. What changed: cycle length, bleeding, sleep, mood, energy, pain, temperature.
3. How long it has been happening: days, weeks or months.
4. What has been checked, and when.

Fill it in before an appointment. It takes less than five minutes and it prevents the most common failure of a short consultation: leaving out the fact that something started six months ago and has been getting worse.

## Questions to take with you

- Given these dates and symptoms, what conditions are on your list?
- What would you expect to see if this were hormonal, and what would you expect if it were not?
- Is there a reason to wait and see, and what would make waiting the wrong choice?
- Which results do you need to see before we decide?

## The standing record

Write down the first date you noticed a change in your cycle, sleep or temperature regulation. Add one thing that was happening in your life that month. The date is more useful than the adjective.
