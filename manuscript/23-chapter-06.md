# Chapter 6: Data as dignity

Rachel kept a spreadsheet. It began as a list of bad days and became a record of dates, sleep hours, cycle days and a column labelled "heart racing". She brought a printed summary to an appointment after two years of leaving with nothing decided.

A dossier can improve an appointment. It cannot make a single test explain two years of symptoms, and it cannot guarantee what a result will be.

## Why a record helps

Clinicians work with limited time and incomplete histories. A person in pain often remembers the worst moment rather than the pattern. A dated record gives both parties something concrete.

Self-tracking has limits. People who track closely sometimes report more anxiety about normal variation. Numbers can become a target rather than a description. Wearable devices differ in accuracy, and consumer sleep scores are estimates. Use the record to support a conversation, not to replace one.

## The standing journal

Keep the daily part under three minutes.

- Cycle day, or calendar date if cycles are irregular or absent.
- Energy from 1 to 10, with a note about what 10 would mean for you.
- Mood from 1 to 10, including irritability or anxiety.
- Stress response: what you could handle that day.
- Sleep: hours, wake-ups and how you felt on waking.
- Digestion: comfort and regularity.
- Physical symptoms: pain, headaches, breast tenderness, bloating, temperature changes.
- Thinking: clear, foggy, or somewhere in between.

Optional fields, if they are useful and not a burden: basal body temperature taken at the same time each morning, resting heart rate, food and medication notes, exercise type and duration.

Once a week, add notable events: a conflict, a deadline, a night of poor sleep, a new prescription.

Once a month, read for patterns. Do symptoms cluster in the ten days before a period? Did a change in medication coincide with a change in sleep? Are the worst days associated with particular nights rather than particular foods?

## The one-page evidence summary

Take the journal to an appointment, but lead with a single page.

1. A three-sentence summary: what has been happening, for how long, and what you would like discussed.
2. A small table or chart with dates along one side and two or three symptoms along the other.
3. A short list of the questions you want answered, in priority order.
4. A medication and supplement list with doses.
5. Any test results you already have, with the date and the lab's reference interval.

Bring research papers only if they are relevant and few in number. Two well-chosen guideline documents are more useful than a folder of printouts. A paper is not evidence that it applies to you; it is a prompt to ask.

## Privacy and safety

Health records are sensitive. Use a lock screen on your phone. Think about who can see a shared calendar or a cloud-synced note. If you are in a situation where your health information could be used against you, a paper record may be safer.

Do not use a consumer period-tracking app as contraception. Analyses of typical calendar-based and temperature-supported apps find that they misclassify fertile days often enough to make them unreliable for preventing pregnancy [1]. They can still be useful for noting cycles and symptoms.

## What a clinician may do with the record

A record can help establish that a symptom is persistent, that it follows a pattern, or that it began after a particular exposure. It can also show that a treatment coincided with a change, or did not.

In the story, Rachel's clinician took the dossier, examined her, arranged a focused set of tests and discussed two possible explanations. The outcome was gradual and involved more than one appointment.

## The trap of self-diagnosis

Search engines will connect any symptom to the most alarming possible cause. A log helps because it adds time and context that a search query lacks. If a pattern worries you, take it to a professional. If it seems to settle, record that as well.

## The standing record

Make a one-page summary using the five items above. Read it aloud once. If you cannot say it in two minutes, cut it until you can.
