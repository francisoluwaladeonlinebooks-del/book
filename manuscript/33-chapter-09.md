# Chapter 9: Recovery and the nervous system

Denise had spent years in a job that required her to stay alert to other people's anger. The vigilance followed her home. She could sit in a parked car outside her house for ten minutes before she could turn off the engine.

She had tried slow-breathing exercises from wellness articles without much success. Promises that a 4-7-8 pattern lowers cortisol by a fixed percentage have no identifiable trial behind them.

Denise found a trauma-informed therapist, started walking most days, used slow breathing when it helped and stopped when it made her more aware of her chest. Progress took months.

## Polyvagal theory, carefully described

Polyvagal theory is a framework about how the autonomic nervous system responds to safety and threat. It has been influential in trauma work. Some of its detailed evolutionary claims are debated among physiologists, and it is not a diagnostic instrument [1]. Clinicians may find its vocabulary useful; patients should not be told they have a "dysregulated nervous system" as if that were a measured finding.

A practical version of the idea is simple: people who have had repeated stress often scan for danger, and that scanning has a cost. Noticing moments when nothing bad is happening can be a deliberate practice. Some writers call these moments "glimmers". The word is fine as a reminder to pay attention; it is not a treatment.

## What slow breathing can and cannot do

Slow breathing can change how a person feels in the short term, and it is low risk for most people. It should not be sold as a way to hit a cortisol target. Home cortisol tests are not a reliable way to grade a breathing practice.

Try a version that does not involve counting if counting makes you tense. Breathe in for a comfortable count, breathe out slightly longer, and continue for a few minutes. Stop if you feel light-headed. People with respiratory conditions should follow their clinician's advice.

## Movement without a performance target

Gentle, regular movement has broad support in public-health guidance. Adults are advised to do 150 to 300 minutes of moderate aerobic activity a week, plus muscle-strengthening work on two or more days [2]. That is a population target, not a demand that someone in pain start running.

For trauma survivors, the question is often how movement feels rather than how hard it is. Walking, swimming, slow strength work, yoga and stretching can all be adapted. A pelvic-floor physical therapist can help when pain or urinary symptoms are part of the picture.

## Building capacity instead of avoiding everything

Avoidance can shrink a life. Denise began with short trips outside alone, then a crowded shop with a friend, then a journey by bus. The steps were chosen because they were possible, not because a chart said she was ready.

This kind of graded return works best with professional support when panic, post-traumatic stress or severe depression is present. If a person has flashbacks, nightmares or intrusive memories, breathwork alone is unlikely to be enough.

## Sleep, alcohol and the evening

Alcohol may reduce the feeling of threat for an hour and fragment sleep later. Caffeine can amplify physical signs of anxiety. These observations are common enough to be worth testing in your own journal: note evening caffeine or alcohol and see what the next night looks like.

## When to get help urgently

Seek immediate help for thoughts of self-harm, a plan to die, hallucinations, or a level of distress that makes it unsafe to be alone. In many countries, an emergency number or a crisis line can connect you to help the same day. Ask a clinician for a mental-health assessment when low mood, loss of interest or anxiety lasts for weeks and interferes with daily life.

## The standing record

Write one situation that felt safe this week and one that felt threatening. Add what your body did in each. Bring the page to a therapist or clinician if you have one.
