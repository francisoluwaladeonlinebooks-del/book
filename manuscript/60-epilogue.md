# Epilogue: your body, your bureau

The claim in this book is modest. It offers a way of keeping track of what happens, so that you can describe it clearly and decide what to do with the answer.

The single-person experiment is worth taking seriously. If you change one habit for a month and record what happened, you know something about your own week that a population study cannot tell you. You also know its limits. One person is one data point. A good n of 1 is not evidence for someone else.

The routine can change as you do. A sleep habit that suited you at 28 may not suit you with a newborn or on nights. A training plan that worked before an injury needs revision. Tracking is a way of noticing when the plan has stopped fitting.

In the closing story, a woman ran a half-marathon at 41. She trained, she had a clinician when she needed one, and she happened to enjoy running. The book gave her a notebook.

If you have read this far, the practical invitation is this: write down three dates and two symptoms before your next appointment. Ask one question you have been putting off. Keep the answer where you can find it.
